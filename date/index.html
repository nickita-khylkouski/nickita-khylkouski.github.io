The idea

Every year has one weekday called its doomsday. A handful of easy dates always fall on it: 4/4, 6/6, 8/8, 10/10, 12/12, the last day of February, and a few more. In 2026 all of them are Saturdays.

Look at the calendar. Every red date sits in the same column.

Change the year and the column moves, but the red dates move together. So to find the weekday of any date, you need two things:

  1. the year’s doomsday, and
  2. how far your date is from the nearest red date.

This is John Conway’s Doomsday rule. Below, it’s split into 5 steps and then put back together. Each step ends with a few questions, and every answer comes with its full working.

1

Give each weekday a number

Adding days is easier with numbers than with names, so number them from Sunday:

Conway gave the numbers names to help them stick: Noneday, Oneday, Twosday, Treblesday, Foursday, Fiveday, Sixaday.

A week is 7 days, so adding 7 lands on the same weekday, and you can throw away 7s whenever you like. Friday plus 4 days is 5 + 4 = 9. Take away 7 and you get 2, which is Tuesday. The short way to write that is 9 mod 7 = 2, the remainder after dividing by 7.

Going backward works the same way. Tuesday minus 4 days is 2 − 4 = −2. Add 7 to get 5, which is Friday.

2

Memorize one doomsday per month

In any year, these dates all land on the same weekday. They come in three groups.

Doubles

4/4, 6/6, 8/8, 10/10, 12/12

The even months from April on. The day matches the month.

Nine to five

5/9, 9/5, 7/11, 11/7

“I work 9-to-5 at the 7-Eleven,” both ways round.

Start of the year

1/3, 2/28, 3/14

January 3, the last day of February, and Pi Day. Leap years use 1/4 and 2/29.

The month lengths are what line them up. From 4/4 to 6/6 is 26 more days in April, 31 in May and 6 in June, which makes 63 days, exactly 9 weeks. The gaps from 6/6 to 8/8, 8/8 to 10/10 and 10/10 to 12/12 are 63 days each too. May 9 to September 5 is 119 days, or 17 weeks, and so is July 11 to November 7.

January and February come before the leap day, so they shift in leap years. Everything from March on stays put.

You don’t have to use the listed date. Anything 7 days from a doomsday is also a doomsday, so in July you can use the 4th, 11th, 18th or 25th, whichever is closest. A few more come free: July 4, Halloween (10/31), Boxing Day (12/26), and Valentine’s Day (2/14) outside leap years.

3

Count from the nearest doomsday

Say you know that 2026’s doomsday is Saturday. What day is Christmas, December 25?

December 12 is a doomsday. Add a week to get December 19, and another to get December 26. Christmas is the day before that, so it’s Saturday minus 1, a Friday.

In numbers, Saturday is 6 and 6 − 1 = 5, which is Friday. Counting from December 12 instead gives the same answer with more arithmetic: 25 − 12 = 13, 6 + 13 = 19, and 19 mod 7 = 5. Starting from a nearby doomsday keeps the numbers small.

4

Find the year’s doomsday

Each year the doomsday moves forward one weekday, or two when a February 29 gets in the way.

A normal year has 365 days, which is 52 weeks and 1 day, so any date falls one weekday later the next year. A leap year adds February 29, so the jump is two.

2026 is Saturday, 2027 is Sunday, and 2028 jumps two days to Tuesday because 2028 is a leap year.

So if you know the doomsday at the start of a century, then after y years it has moved y days, plus one extra for every leap year along the way: y + ⌊y/4⌋. The ⌊ ⌋ brackets mean “round down.”

For 1969, y = 69. There are 17 leap years from 1901 to 1969, so the drift is 69 + 17 = 86 days. Throw away 7s: 86 = 84 + 2, which leaves 2. The 1900s start on Wednesday (3), for reasons you’ll see in step 5, so 1969’s doomsday is 3 + 2 = 5, a Friday.

The 12s shortcut

86 mod 7 is a lot to do in your head. Grouping the years in twelves keeps the numbers small. Any 12 years in a row contain exactly 3 leap years, so 12 years of drift is 12 + 3 = 15 days, which is 2 weeks and 1 day. Each dozen years moves the doomsday by one.

Split the last two digits into dozens and a leftover, then add three small numbers:

  1. a = how many 12s fit into y
  2. b = what’s left over
  3. c = how many 4s fit into b (the leap years in the leftover)

The drift is a + b + c. For 1969, 69 = 5 × 12 + 9, so a = 5 and b = 9. Two 4s fit into 9, so c = 2. Then 5 + 9 + 2 = 16, and 16 mod 7 = 2, the same drift as before.

5

Know the century anchors

The last piece is the doomsday of each century year, called its anchor. The anchors repeat every 400 years:

For most dates you’ll meet, you need two of them: 1900 is Wednesday and 2000 is Tuesday (2000 is Twosday).

Each century moves the anchor back two days. A century normally has 24 leap years, because the century year itself, like 1900, isn’t one. That’s 100 + 24 = 124 days of drift, and 124 = 17 weeks + 5 days. Moving forward 5 lands on the same weekday as moving back 2, so each anchor is two days before the last: Sunday in 1700, Friday in 1800, Wednesday in 1900.

Every fourth century year is a leap year anyway (1600, 2000, 2400). That adds one more day, so the step into 2000 is only one day back, from Wednesday to Tuesday. Over 400 years that’s 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 7 days back, a whole week, which is why the pattern repeats. 400 years of our calendar are 146,097 days, exactly 20,871 weeks, so the entire calendar repeats every 400 years.

A formula, if you’d like one

With c = the first two digits of the year, the anchor is (2 + 5 × (c mod 4)) mod 7. For 1969, c = 19, 19 mod 4 = 3, and (2 + 15) mod 7 = 3, which is Wednesday.

What trips people up

January and February in leap years
Use January 4 and February 29 in leap years. A year divisible by 4 is a leap year, except century years, which must be divisible by 400. So 1900 and 2100 aren’t leap years, but 2000 was.
Counting backward
If your date comes before the doomsday, count back. If you end up with a negative number, add 7s until it isn’t: −11 + 14 = 3.
Which century?
Here “century” means the first two digits. 1999 is in the 1900s (Wednesday) and 2000 is in the 2000s (Tuesday).
Very old dates
Countries moved from the Julian calendar to today’s Gregorian one at different times. Britain and its colonies switched in 1752, when Wednesday, September 2 was followed by Thursday, September 14. The weekdays carried on and only the dates jumped. This method uses the Gregorian calendar, so an old-style date won’t match until you convert it.

Get fast

Conway practiced until he could do it in about 2 seconds, and set his computer to quiz him with a random date every time he logged in. A target of 10 seconds is a good start. You get there by drilling each step until you stop having to think about it.
